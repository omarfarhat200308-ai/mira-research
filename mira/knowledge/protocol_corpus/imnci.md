## General danger signs in young children

A child who cannot drink or breastfeed, vomits everything they are given,
has had convulsions during the current illness, or is unusually lethargic
or difficult to wake is considered to have a general danger sign. Any one
of these signs alone is enough to treat the case as needing urgent
in-person assessment, regardless of what any single symptom on its own
would suggest.

## Fast breathing as a marker of possible pneumonia in children

Breathing rate is one of the most reliable low-cost signs available to a
community health worker without equipment. A child breathing noticeably
faster than normal for their age, especially together with cough, should
be considered for urgent referral rather than managed as a routine cough.
Chest indrawing (the lower chest wall pulling in with each breath) is a
more severe sign than fast breathing alone and raises urgency further.

## Diarrhea and dehydration assessment

Not all diarrhea needs referral. The distinguishing factor is dehydration
signs: sunken eyes, skin that stays pinched when pressed (poor skin
turgor), unusual thirst or inability to drink, and lethargy. A child with
loose stools but none of these signs, who is alert and drinking normally,
can typically be managed with oral rehydration and monitored locally
rather than referred urgently.

## Fever in a young child

Fever alone, in a child who is otherwise alert, feeding, and playful, is
usually not itself a danger sign. Fever becomes higher-priority when
combined with any general danger sign, with a stiff neck or bulging soft
spot on the head (possible meningitis pattern), or when it persists for
many days without an obvious cause. Age matters: fever in a very young
infant (under about 2 months) is treated more cautiously than the same
fever in an older child, because young infants can deteriorate quickly
without showing other obvious warning signs first.

## Newborn-specific warning signs

Newborns can look deceptively well shortly before deteriorating quickly.
Signs that should prompt urgent referral include: not feeding well or
stopping feeding, feeling cold to the touch (poor thermoregulation),
unusual sleepiness or difficulty waking for feeds, fast or difficult
breathing, and a swollen or discharging umbilical stump. Because the
clinical picture in newborns changes fast, "wait and watch" is a riskier
default than it is for an older child.

## When cough or breathing problems need urgent referral vs. routine follow-up

A cough of a few days with no fast breathing, no chest indrawing, and a
child who remains active and feeding well is typically routine. A cough
lasting multiple weeks, especially with weight loss or persistent low-grade
fever, is not itself a danger sign but warrants evaluation for causes that
a brief illness would not explain (e.g. tuberculosis workup in the relevant
population), so it is treated as urgent-but-not-emergency rather than
purely routine.
